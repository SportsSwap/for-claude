### 1.4 What drove the 2023–2025 crash

- 2022's price spike triggered demand destruction in China; battery and vehicle makers stopped panic-buying and drew down inflated inventories (carboncredits.com / internationalbanker.com, via WebSearch).
- New supply funded during the 2021–22 boom came online just as Chinese EV subsidy changes cooled demand growth, flipping the market from deficit to surplus.
- S&P Global Commodity Insights: global surplus of **120,000t LCE in 2023**, **84,000t LCE in 2024** (see §4 for full balance table).
- Continued oversupply through 2024 pushed China domestic carbonate to a near-3-year low (72,250 CNY/t, 9 Sep 2024) and then to the cycle trough of US$9,550/t CIF North Asia in February 2025 (S&P Global, via carboncredits.com).

### 1.5 What has driven the 2025–2026 recovery

Named, dated catalysts identified in research:

1. **CATL's Jianxiawo (Yichun, Jiangxi) lepidolite mine suspension** — initially halted 9 August 2025 after its mining permit expired; the largest lithium operation in China by nameplate capacity (~150,000 tpa LCE). See §2.2 for full timeline.
2. **Jiangxi provincial mining-licence crackdown** — the Yichun Bureau of Natural Resources proposed cancelling 27 further mining permits (Benchmark Mineral Intelligence, via WebSearch); the wider province produces an estimated ~108,000t LCE/year from operations other than Jianxiawo, all now under heightened regulatory risk.
3. **Zimbabwe's raw-mineral/concentrate export ban**, accelerated to 25 February 2026 (originally flagged for January 2027) — see §2.2.
4. **Structural demand growth from battery energy storage (BESS)**, up 51% in 2025 (pv-magazine/ESS News, 20 Jan 2026) and accelerating into 2026 partly on AI-datacentre power demand.
5. **Falling Chinese inventories** — SMM weekly aggregate lithium carbonate inventory stood at 92,236t as of 8 July 2026, down 2,337t week-on-week, with total industry days-of-inventory cover reported to have fallen below one month in early-2026 commentary (Mysteel/SMM, via WebSearch).

### 2.2 Supply discipline events

**CATL Jianxiawo / Yichun (Jiangxi, China) — lepidolite:**
- Nameplate capacity ~150,000 tpa LCE — the largest single lithium operation in China.
- **9 Aug 2025:** suspended after its mining permit expired.
- **29 June 2026:** restart permit reissued, valid to 27 Feb 2028.
- **July 2026:** brief operational restart — but this bypassed an unresolved tailings-pond dispute that had drawn 70+ formal complaints.
- **First week of August 2026:** authorities halted the operation again, forcing CATL back into the environmental impact assessment (EIA) process.
- **As of Sept 2026:** back on care-and-maintenance; the EIA review "may not conclude until Q4 2026 or could extend into 2027."
- Benchmark Mineral Intelligence cut its 2026 production estimate for the site to **32,000t LCE**, down sharply from an earlier projection of **62,500t**.
- Sources: Caixin Global (8 Aug 2026), Benchmark Mineral Intelligence, mining.com, all via WebSearch synthesis.

**Jiangxi mining-licence crackdown (broader):**
- Yichun Bureau of Natural Resources proposed cancelling **27 mining permits** (all already expired, some lapsed more than a decade) following public consultation.
- Wider Jiangxi province projected to produce ~108,000t LCE in 2026 from operations other than Jianxiawo — all now exposed to the same inspection regime.
- Source: Benchmark Mineral Intelligence ("What do the Jiangxi permit cancellations mean for lithium?"), via WebSearch.

**Zimbabwe:**
- **25 Feb 2026:** government suspended ALL exports of unprocessed minerals, including lithium-bearing concentrates — indefinite ("until further notice"), covering material already in transit. Processed products (carbonate/hydroxide) remain exportable.
- This accelerated an earlier, less aggressive timeline: in late 2022 Zimbabwe had banned raw ore exports and flagged a concentrate export ban for **January 2027** — the 2026 action moved this forward by nearly a year.
- Bikita mine: acquired by Sinomine Resource Group for US$180m (July 2023); world's largest-known lithium deposit (~11Mt); dual processing plant capacity up to 300,000 tpa spodumene concentrate + 480,000 tpa petalite.
- Arcadia Mine: open-pit, near Harare — described as the world's biggest hard-rock lithium resource.
- Sources: Al Jazeera (25 Feb 2026), McCarthy Tétrault, African Climate Wire, ChemAnalyst, all via WebSearch.

**Mali — Goulamina:**
- Operated via JV between Ganfeng Lithium and (formerly) Leo Lithium; Ganfeng holds an indirect ~55% stake in Mali Lithium B.V.
- Started operations late 2024.

### 2.4 Project pipeline 2026–2030 and incentive economics

- **Rio Tinto Rincon (Salta, Argentina; DLE):** US$2.5bn investment announced December 2024, targeting **60,000 tpa** battery-grade lithium carbonate capacity; US$1.175bn financing package secured March 2026 from four international lenders; first production targeted **2028**, with a 3-year ramp to full capacity. Post-Arcadium-acquisition, Rio Tinto's combined Argentine portfolio (Olaroz, Fenix, Sal de Vida, Cauchari, plus Rincon) totals ~56.6Mt LCE of resources.
- Approximately **45% of projected 2035 demand** will need to be met by projects not yet in construction (source attribution unclear — flagged as a single, unverified secondary citation).
- Peak investment window for new supply estimated at **2030–2034**, a **US$100–275bn** investment story depending on the energy-transition scenario (unclear primary attribution — flagged).
- Benchmark Mineral Intelligence: lithium industry needs **>US$116bn** to meet automaker and policy targets by 2030; **>300 new lithium projects** required by 2035 to meet forecast demand.
- IEA: mined supply from currently announced projects falls short of 2035 demand by an implied **40%** under the STEPS scenario.
- Wood Mackenzie (Lithium Energy Transition Outlook, published 3 March 2026): existing supply projects are unlikely to meet demand beyond the mid-2030s under its base case; a 2030–35 supply gap of **20–30%** is cited elsewhere against the current project pipeline (secondary source, flagged).

### 3.3 LFP vs NMC chemistry mix

- China, 2025: LFP cells = **625.3 GWh (81.2% of China's EV battery market)**, +52.9% YoY; NMC = **144.1 GWh (18.7%)**.
- LFP reached roughly half of the *global* EV market by 2024, and now powers roughly two-thirds of EV sales in emerging-market/developing economies (double the 2023 share), driven by Chinese vehicle/battery exports.
- Forward view: if LFP share continues toward **~75% of Li-ion batteries by 2030**, NMC cell demand may have already peaked in 2023 (WebSearch synthesis of PatentPC/Thunder Said Energy-style commentary).
- **Read-through for the lithium supply chain:** LFP uses lithium carbonate (not hydroxide) and is spodumene/lepidolite/brine-carbonate-route agnostic; continued LFP share gains mechanically shift incremental lithium demand growth toward carbonate relative to hydroxide, reinforcing the carbonate-price-cycle centrality shown in §1 and reducing relative reliance on nickel/cobalt-linked NMC economics.

### 3.5 Recycling outlook

- IEA (Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025 / Recycling of Critical Minerals report, via WebSearch): recycling could supply **10–15% of critical minerals generally by 2035**; for lithium/nickel/cobalt specifically, battery recycling could meet **20–30% of demand by 2050** — contingent on improving collection rates.
- Separately, the IEA is cited as projecting that by **2040**, secondary/recycled production accounts for **~5% of total lithium supply** (vs ~12% for cobalt, ~7% for nickel) — this figure appears to come from a different IEA analysis than the 20–30%-by-2050 figure above; **the two data points are not obviously reconcilable on a single trajectory and are presented separately rather than blended.**
- Manufacturing scrap (not genuine end-of-life recycling) is expected to account for **two-thirds of available recycling feedstock through 2030** — meaning real end-of-life battery recycling remains a marginal supply source until the early-2030s retirement wave of the first mass-market EV cohort.
- **Practical takeaway for the pitch: recycling is not a material offset to primary mine supply before the early-to-mid 2030s**, consistent across all recycling-specific sources found.

### 3.6 Sodium-ion and solid-state substitution risk

**Sodium-ion:**
- Commercial in 2026: CATL and BYD both ramping GWh-scale production; three cathode chemistries reaching maturity; cost parity with LFP projected by **2027**.
- February 2026: the world's first mass-produced sodium-ion passenger vehicle launched — a Changan Automobile/CATL joint development.
- CATL confirmed at its December 2025 supplier conference that sodium-ion batteries would enter large-scale deployment in 2026 across battery-swapping, passenger vehicles, commercial vehicles, and energy storage.
- Positioning: sodium-ion is broadly described (WebSearch synthesis) as a **strategic complement, not a wholesale replacement** — targeting cost-sensitive mass-market vehicles and stationary storage, not premium/long-range applications.

**Solid-state:**
- CATL and BYD targeting limited solid-state production around **2027**; broader commercialisation (pilots, first shipments) expected **2027–2028**; mass production in mainstream automotive largely clustered around **2030** (consensus view across sources).
- A more cautious view cited: early products late 2020s, wide-scale adoption only in the 2030s.
- **Important distinction for the pitch:** most solid-state battery chemistries still use lithium (as the active material/electrolyte carrier) — solid-state is primarily a *format and safety/energy-density* shift, not a lithium-substitution technology in the way sodium-ion is. The realistic substitution risk to primary lithium demand this decade is concentrated in sodium-ion penetration of the cost-sensitive EV/two-wheeler/stationary-storage segments, not solid-state.
- Net assessment: **no credible source reviewed suggests either technology materially displaces lithium demand before 2030**; sodium-ion is the more direct competitive risk but is capacity- and cost-curve-constrained through the rest of this decade.

## 5. Structural / policy

### 5.1 Australia

- **Critical Minerals Strategy 2023–2030** (announced mid-2023): sets out a framework for growing Australia's critical-minerals sector, including domestic funding facilities for exploration/production and international partnerships.
- **Future Made in Australia** (2024–25 Federal Budget): **A$22.7bn** committed over the decade to maximise the economic/industrial benefits of the net-zero transition.
- **Critical Minerals Production Tax Incentive (CMPTI)** — legislated under Division 419 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997:
  - **10% refundable tax offset** on eligible processing expenditure, **no maximum claimable amount**.
  - Available **1 July 2027 to 30 June 2040** (max 10 years per claimant).
  - Refundable — can generate a cash refund, not just an offset against tax otherwise payable.
  - Jointly administered by the Department of Industry, Science and Resources (which registers eligible "CMPTI processing activities") and the ATO.
  - **A "CMPTI processing activity" requires substantial transformation** of a critical-mineral-bearing feedstock into a purer/more refined, chemically distinct form.
  - **Critical nuance for a lithium pitch: standard 5.5–6% Li2O spodumene concentrate, as typically exported by Australian miners, is explicitly NOT eligible.** Only further downstream processing (e.g., conversion to lithium hydroxide or carbonate) qualifies — meaning the CMPTI rewards Australian midstream refining capacity (e.g., a hydroxide plant), not raw concentrate exports, and is not a direct offset for a pure spodumene-concentrate producer's economics.
  - 2024–25 Federal Budget estimated the CMPTI's value at **A$7bn** (cost to the budget over the forward estimates / support value across the scheme).
- **Australian Critical Minerals Prospectus** (released Feb 2026): profiled **78 investment-ready projects** from 60 companies, including **1 lithium project**; also listed 29 midstream processing opportunities, **4 of which were lithium-related**.
- **Export earnings:** REQ (June 2026) forecasts Australia's lithium export earnings rising from **A$9.9bn in 2025–26 to A$12.5bn in 2026–27** (real terms), on the back of the price recovery.

### 5.2 European Union

- **Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA)** — now in force. 2030 targets: **≥10% of EU consumption extracted domestically, ≥40% processed domestically, ≥25% recycled domestically**; and **no single third country to supply more than 65%** of the EU's annual consumption of any strategic raw material.
- **Strategic Projects:** batches of projects have been designated with financial/permitting support — search results referenced both "47 industrial projects" (one year after CRMA adoption) and a separate "60 strategic projects" headline; **these may reflect different designation rounds and were not reconciled to a single confirmed count.** Of the batch most clearly documented: lithium accounts for **22 projects** (the largest single-material count), ahead of graphite (11), nickel (12), cobalt (10), manganese (7). Implementation window: 25 March 2025 to 2030; total capital investment across the batch estimated at **€22.5bn**.
- EU lithium demand is projected to rise **12x by 2030** and **21x by 2050** (baseline year not specified in source) as battery/EV manufacturing scales domestically.
- **EU Battery Regulation (2023/1542) — carbon footprint & due-diligence timeline (the key "green premium" mechanism):**

| Date | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Feb 2025 | Carbon footprint declaration requirements begin for EV batteries |
| 18 Aug 2027 | Due-diligence obligations begin for in-scope economic operators (covering cobalt, natural graphite, lithium, nickel) — postponed from an original Aug 2025 date |
| Aug 2026 | Carbon footprint **performance classes** (A–E-style grading) must be declared for EV batteries |
| 18 Feb 2027 | Carbon footprint declarations must become accessible via QR code |
| Feb 2028 | Carbon footprint **maximum thresholds** start to apply for EV batteries — batteries above the threshold can be excluded from the EU market |
| 18 Aug 2028 | Mandatory disclosure of recycled-content percentages (cobalt, lead, lithium, nickel) in active materials |

**This is the single most concrete, dated regulatory lever for a "green premium" argument in the pitch deck**: carbon-intensity grading is already required from this year (Aug 2026), with a hard cap following in Feb 2028. Lithium chemicals produced from Australian spodumene on a renewables-leaning grid start from a structurally lower carbon base than Chinese lepidolite processed on a coal-heavy grid — a gap that becomes commercially binding, not just disclosed, once the Feb 2028 threshold takes effect.

### 5.3 United States

- **One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA):** EV purchase tax credits (up to US$7,500) sunset **September 2025**; EV charging-infrastructure incentives sunset by **June 2026**.
- **Tariffs (from 2 Feb 2025):** 145% duty on Chinese EVs and battery materials; 93.5% tariff on Chinese graphite.
- **Section 301:** Chinese LFP cells face a 64.9% tariff (2025), rising to **82.4% in 2026**.
- Estimated market effect: Chinese-made LFP cell prices could rise from ~US$60–70/kWh (2024) to **>US$100/kWh** (2025–26) — back to roughly 2022 levels.
- Net demand effect: US EV sales fell 4% in 2025 and are forecast to fall a further ~19% in 2026 (BloombergNEF) — the only major region contracting, directly attributable to policy withdrawal. Eliminating EV tax credits alone is estimated to reduce the 2030 EV share of new US vehicle sales by 6 percentage points over 2026–2035 (WebSearch synthesis of a Harvard Salata Institute policy brief).
- **Pitch read-through:** US policy retrenchment is a genuine demand headwind, but it is concentrated in one region (which is also NMC/hydroxide-weighted relative to China/Europe's LFP-heavy mix) — it dents the global total less than a naive "US policy risk" framing might suggest, while reinforcing the LFP/carbonate-centric structural demand story out of China, Europe, and the Rest-of-World cohort.

### 5.4 China — export controls / resource nationalism

- **9 Oct 2025:** China imposed export controls on five additional rare-earth elements plus a broad set of related materials/technologies, with extraterritorial reach to products containing as little as **0.1% Chinese-origin content**.
- **7 Nov 2025:** those controls were **suspended for one year** as part of the Xi–Trump agreement to roll back tariffs and trade barriers.
- **Important scope note:** the confirmed October 2025 export-control action targeted **rare earths**, not lithium specifically. **No lithium-specific Chinese export control was found/verified in this research** — the operative China-side lithium story for 2025–2026 is domestic supply-side tightening (the Jianxiawo suspension and Jiangxi licence crackdown in §2.2), not export restriction on lithium itself. This distinction matters and should not be blurred in the pitch.
- Background: China's "Made in China 2025" industrial policy and successors have long identified lithium (alongside rare earths, cobalt, graphite) as strategic for domestic high-tech self-sufficiency — context for why Beijing tolerates near-term domestic supply discipline (which supports prices) even though it constrains China's own processors.

### 5.5 Japan / Korea supply-chain de-risking

**NOT FOUND.** No specific, dated 2025–2026 JOGMEC or Korean-government lithium stockpiling, offtake, or financing agreement could be verified within the available research budget. This should be treated as a genuine research gap, not as evidence of no activity — Japan and Korea have well-documented general critical-minerals de-risking policies, but this report cannot cite specific lithium-related deals without further, dedicated research.

### 5.6 Non-China / "green" supply premium

- Western and non-Chinese lithium (and graphite) producers have begun charging the EV supply chain premiums for ESG-friendly, traceable, non-China-linked supply — a trend documented as early as November 2023 (MiningWeekly/MarketScreener) and understood to be continuing into the 2025–2026 de-risking push.
- **Concrete example:** Sigma Lithium (Brazil) sold its 10th shipment of "Quintuple Zero Green Lithium" (22,000t) at a floating premium of **8.75% over the LME lithium hydroxide reference price** (date of this specific shipment not precisely pinned down in research, but reported in the context of an ongoing programme).
- Rationale cited: Western governments (US, EU, Japan) and OEMs want supply not linked to China's weaker environmental standards, mirroring a similar rare-earths dynamic.
- **This directly complements the EU Battery Regulation carbon-footprint mechanism (§5.2):** the commercial premium already being paid for "green" lithium today is likely to become more structurally embedded once EU carbon-class disclosure (Aug 2026) and thresholds (Feb 2028) are live, rather than being a purely voluntary/marketing premium.

## 6. Summary of the strongest, most defensible bull arguments (for pitch use)

Cross-referencing the evidence above, the three best-evidenced arguments that lithium prices rise over 2026–2028 are:

1. **A concrete, dated, and re-escalating supply shock in China's own domestic lithium base.** The CATL Jianxiawo mine — ~150,000 tpa LCE nameplate, the largest lithium operation in China — has now been suspended, restarted, and re-suspended twice within 13 months (Aug 2025 → Jun/Jul 2026 restart → early Aug 2026 re-suspension), with Benchmark Mineral Intelligence cutting its 2026 output estimate for the site by nearly half (62,500t → 32,000t LCE) and the review possibly not concluding until 2027. This sits alongside a further ~108,000 tpa LCE of Jiangxi capacity under the same regulatory scrutiny. This is not a hypothetical modelling assumption — it is an ongoing, named, dated event actively removing verified tonnes from the market.
2. **A second, independent supply-side shock from Zimbabwe**, whose government banned all raw mineral/concentrate exports indefinitely from 25 February 2026 — nearly a year earlier than the market had priced in (concentrate exports were expected to remain legal until January 2027). Two unrelated, sovereign-risk-driven supply disruptions (China's regulatory tightening, Zimbabwe's resource-nationalist export ban) hitting in the same 12-month window is a materially stronger and more diversified bull case than either alone.
3. **A genuine, structural second demand pillar (BESS) is now large enough to move the market on its own, and is still accelerating.** Battery storage grew installations ~51% in 2025 (on top of 26% EV battery demand growth the same year) and BESS's share of total lithium demand is estimated to have grown from ~23% (2025) to ~31% (2026e), partly pulled by AI-datacentre power demand — a demand driver that is structurally independent of the EV-purchase-incentive cycle that is currently working against the market in the US. This diversifies lithium demand away from single-point EV-policy risk (the US IRA/tariff rollback under the Trump administration) at exactly the point that US EV demand is contracting, which is why global demand growth (Reuters poll: +17–30% in 2026) has kept climbing through the US policy headwind rather than being derailed by it.

Corroborating context: named forecasters have moved decisively in the same direction over 2026 — Fastmarkets raised its 2027 carbonate forecast from US$22.65/kg to US$31.40/kg; MinRes and IGO's realised SC6 prices roughly tripled from the 2025 trough (~US$600–750/t) to US$2,100–2,425/t by mid-2026; and the Australian Government's own REQ (June 2026) now forecasts a full-year 2026 spodumene average of US$2,236/t. The main counter-evidence to flag honestly: Wood Mackenzie's March 2026 base case still calls for a lithium-chemicals surplus in 2026–27 and pushes its base-case deficit out to "the early 2030s" — a genuine, credible dissenting view that should be acknowledged rather than ignored in the pitch.
